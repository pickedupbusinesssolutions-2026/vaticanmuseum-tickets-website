The Vatican Gardens are the quiet green half of Vatican City that most visitors never see. While crowds pack the museums and the basilica, the gardens sit peacefully behind them, a mix of lawns, woods, fountains, and formal flowerbeds where popes have walked for centuries.

The catch is that you cannot just stroll in. The gardens are a private part of Vatican City, open only to visitors on an organised tour.

This guide explains what they are, whether you can visit, how to do it, what it costs, and what you will see.

What are the Vatican Gardens?

The Vatican Gardens, or Giardini Vaticani in Italian, are the landscaped grounds that fill most of Vatican City, covering around 23 hectares, more than half of the whole country.

They have been here since the 13th century, when the popes planted an orchard and lawn behind the papal residence, and they have been added to ever since.

Today they are a patchwork of styles: neat Italian formal gardens, an English-style landscape, a French area, shady woodland, and rows of fountains, all dotted with monuments and small buildings. They are also a working green space and a private retreat for the pope.

Where are the Vatican Gardens?

The gardens lie on the hill behind St. Peter's Basilica, filling the western half of Vatican City. From inside the Vatican Museums you can glimpse parts of them from the windows and courtyards, but the entrance to the gardens themselves is not on the public museum route.

Because they sit behind the basilica, the gardens offer views of St. Peter's dome that almost no other visitors get, seen from the back and from among the trees.

Can you visit the Vatican Gardens?

Yes, but only on a tour. The gardens are not a public park, so you cannot walk in off the street or wander on your own. Access is limited to visitors who have booked an official guided tour or open-bus tour in advance.

This is the answer to the two most common questions. You cannot visit the Vatican Gardens without a tour, and they are not open to the public in the way the square or the basilica are. The upside is that a tour is easy to book, and it comes with a guide or audio commentary that explains what you are seeing.

How to visit the Vatican Gardens

There are two main ways to see the gardens, both booked ahead:

  • Guided walking tour - A guide walks you through the gardens for around two hours, which is the most in-depth way to see them. Group sizes are limited, so these sell out early.
  • Open-bus tour - An open-top minibus loops through the gardens in about 45 minutes with audio commentary, which is quicker and good if you are short on time or energy.

Both options almost always include entry to the Vatican Museums and Sistine Chapel, so you do the gardens first and then go straight into the museums.

Are the Vatican Gardens Free?

No. There is no free entry to the Vatican Gardens, because the only way in is a paid tour. Even the quickest open-bus option costs a tour fee, and the walking tours cost more.

What you are paying for is access to a private part of Vatican City plus, in most cases, your Vatican Museums and Sistine Chapel ticket rolled into the same booking. So while the gardens are not free, the price usually covers the main Vatican visit as well.

What you See in the Gardens

The tours take in the highlights of the grounds, which include:

  • The fountains - Several grand fountains, including the Galleon Fountain, a bronze model ship that fires water, and the Fountain of the Eagle.
  • The Lourdes Grotto - A replica of the famous French grotto, given to the pope and set among the trees.
  • Formal gardens and woodland - Clipped Italian parterres, open lawns, and shaded woods, changing in style as you move through.
  • Monuments and buildings - Including the elegant Casina of Pius IV, old fortified walls, and views across to the Governorate building.
  • Views of St. Peter's - The back of the basilica and its dome, seen from angles the crowds never get.

Is the Vatican Gardens Tour Worth it?

For most first-time visitors with limited time, the museums, Sistine Chapel, and basilica come first, and the gardens are an extra.

But if you have seen the Vatican before, love gardens, or simply want a calm, green break from the crowds, the tour is a lovely and unusual way to spend a morning.

The open-bus tour is the easy add-on, while the walking tour suits anyone who wants the full story and does not mind a slower pace. Either way you also get your museum entry, which softens the cost.

Visiting Tips

  • Book early - Garden tours run in limited numbers and sell out, especially the walking tours, so reserve well ahead.
  • Go in the morning - Tours run in the morning, which is also the coolest and freshest time in the gardens.
  • Check the weather - The gardens are outdoors, so a wet day is less pleasant, and wear comfortable shoes for the walking tour.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you visit the Vatican Gardens?

Yes, you can visit the Vatican Gardens but only on a booked tour, either a guided walking tour or an open-bus tour. You cannot walk in on your own, as the gardens are a private part of Vatican City.

Are the Vatican Gardens free?

No. The only way to visit Vatican Gardens is through a paid tour, so there is no free entry. The tour price also includes your Vatican Museums and Sistine Chapel ticket.

Can you visit the Vatican Gardens without a tour?

No. There is no independent entry to the Vatican Gardens. Access is limited to official guided and open-bus tours booked in advance.

How do you visit the Vatican Gardens?

Book a guided walking tour or an open-bus tour through the official Vatican ticket office or a licensed operator. Both usually include entry to the museums and Sistine Chapel.

How long does a Vatican Gardens tour take?

The open-bus tour takes about 45 minutes, and the guided walking tour around two hours, not counting the museum visit that usually follows.

What is in the Vatican Gardens?

Formal Italian gardens, woodland, lawns, grand fountains, a replica of the Lourdes grotto, historic buildings like the Casina of Pius IV, and some of the best views of St. Peter's dome.
