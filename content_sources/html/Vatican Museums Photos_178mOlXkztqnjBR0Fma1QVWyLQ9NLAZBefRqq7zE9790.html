Vatican Museums Photos: A Visual Walkthrough

Quick answer: The Vatican Museums are one of the most photogenic places in Rome. The best-known shots are the double spiral staircase at the exit, the golden Gallery of Maps, the Pinecone Courtyard with its bronze sphere, and the Raphael Rooms. You can take photos in the general galleries for personal use, but with no flash, and photography is not allowed at all in the Sistine Chapel or on the Necropolis tour. Can You Take Photos in the Vatican Museums?

Mostly yes. You are allowed to take photos in the general galleries and courtyards for personal use, as long as you do not use flash, which is banned throughout. Tripods and selfie sticks are also not allowed.

There are two firm exceptions. No photos or video of any kind are allowed inside the Sistine Chapel, where guards enforce it and ask you to put your phone away. And the Vatican Necropolis, the ancient burial ground under St. Peter's seen on the Scavi tour, bans photography entirely, which is why genuine Vatican Necropolis photos are so rare online.

The Pinecone Courtyard

[Photo: the Pinecone Courtyard, with the giant bronze pinecone and the modern Sphere Within Sphere sculpture]

One of the first open spaces on the route, the Pinecone Courtyard (Cortile della Pigna) is named after the huge first-century bronze pinecone at one end. In the middle sits Arnaldo Pomodoro's modern bronze Sphere Within Sphere, which slowly rotates.

It is a good place to catch your breath and take a wide shot, and it photographs well because it is outdoors and less crowded than the galleries.

The Gallery of Maps

[Photo: the Gallery of Maps, the long corridor with painted maps and a gilded ceiling]

The single most photographed room in the museums. The Gallery of Maps is a 120-metre corridor lined with 16th-century painted maps of Italy, under a dazzling gold and fresco ceiling.

It is the "golden tunnel" you see all over social media. It gets crowded fast, so for a clean shot you want to be here early. Our Gallery of Maps guide has the detail.

The Raphael Rooms

[Photo: the Raphael Rooms, with the fresco of the School of Athens]

Four rooms painted by Raphael and his workshop, the highlight being the School of Athens, with its crowd of ancient philosophers under soaring arches. The frescoes cover whole walls, so they reward a wide shot. Our Raphael Rooms guide covers what you are looking at.

The Sistine Chapel

[Photo: NOT ALLOWED inside. Use an official or licensed image of Michelangelo's ceiling only, or a photo of the chapel exterior, and do not imply the photo was taken inside]

The Sistine Chapel is the climax of the visit, with Michelangelo's ceiling and the Last Judgment on the altar wall. It is also the one place you cannot photograph, so the only images you will have are from books, postcards, or official sources.

In person, the move is to simply look up and take it in. Our Sistine Chapel guide describes what to look for.

The Spiral Staircase

[Photo: the double-helix Momo spiral staircase, shot looking down from the top]

The famous double spiral staircase, designed by Giuseppe Momo in 1932, is at the exit. Two intertwined ramps spiral down so that people going up never meet those coming down, and shot from above it makes one of the most striking photos in the whole Vatican. Have your camera ready as you leave, as this is the last thing you see.

St. Peter's Basilica and Square

[Photo: St. Peter's Basilica dome and the colonnade of St. Peter's Square]

Just beyond the museums, St. Peter's Basilica and the vast oval of St. Peter's Square are among Rome's great views, especially the dome from the square or from the top of the dome itself. Photography is allowed inside the Basilica without flash. Our St. Peter's Basilica tickets guide covers visiting and the dome climb.

Best Photo Spots and Tips

A few things help you come away with better Vatican Museums photos:

  • Go early - The first slot at opening is the only realistic time to photograph the Gallery of Maps or the staircase without a crowd in the frame. Our best time to visit guide explains the quiet windows.
  • No flash, no tripod - Flash is banned everywhere and tripods and selfie sticks are not allowed, so use a steady hand and a phone or camera that handles low light.
  • The staircase is the money shot - Shoot it looking straight down from the top for the full spiral effect.
  • Respect the Sistine Chapel - No photos at all, so do not try, as guards will stop you.
  • Courtyards are your friend  - The Pinecone and Belvedere courtyards are outdoors, bright, and less crowded, so they are easier to shoot than the dim galleries.

Once you have planned your shots, book a timed skip-the-line entry ticket so you get in early enough to catch the quiet rooms.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you take photos in the Vatican Museums?

Yes, in the general galleries and courtyards for personal use, but without flash, tripods, or selfie sticks. Photography is not allowed in the Sistine Chapel or on the Vatican Necropolis tour.

Why can't you take photos in the Sistine Chapel?

It is a consecrated space, and the rule also helps protect the frescoes and keep the room calm. Guards enforce the no-photo rule and will ask you to put your phone away.

What is the most photographed spot in the Vatican Museums?

The Gallery of Maps, a long golden corridor of painted maps, and the double spiral staircase at the exit are the two most photographed. Both look best early, before the crowds.

Are there photos of the Vatican Necropolis?

Very few real ones, because photography is banned on the Scavi tour of the Necropolis. Any images you find are usually official or from books, not taken by visitors.

Can I take photos inside St. Peter's Basilica?

Yes, without flash. The Basilica, its dome, and St. Peter's Square are all among the best photo spots at the Vatican.
