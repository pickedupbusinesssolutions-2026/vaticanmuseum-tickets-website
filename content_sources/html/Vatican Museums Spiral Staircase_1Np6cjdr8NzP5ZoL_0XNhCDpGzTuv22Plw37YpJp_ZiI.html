What is the Spiral Staircase?

The spiral staircase is a wide, gently curving ramp that winds down through several floors at the exit of the Vatican Museums. It is not really a set of steps but a smooth, shallow slope, edged with a decorative bronze railing, built so that it was once easy for people and even carts to move up and down.

It is best known for its shape. Two separate spirals twist around the same central space, one leading up and one leading down, like the strands of a DNA helix. Seen from above or below, they form a hypnotic pattern that has become one of the signature images of the Vatican.

Where is the Spiral Staircase in the Vatican Museums?

The staircase is at the very end of the visit, near the exit. After you have walked the galleries and finished in the Sistine Chapel, your route brings you back toward the entrance hall, and the staircase is the last thing you descend before stepping outside.

Because it is at the exit, you do not need to go looking for it, as everyone passes it on the way out. There is no separate ticket and nothing to book, since it is simply part of the one-way museum route.

Who Designed the Staircase and When

The staircase was designed by the Italian architect Giuseppe Momo and built in 1932. It was created as a grand new entrance and exit for the museums, able to handle large numbers of visitors moving in both directions at once.

Momo took his inspiration from a much older staircase in the Vatican, the Bramante Staircase of 1505, and gave the idea a modern, sweeping form. The result is often loosely called the Bramante Staircase too, which causes some confusion, since there are really two different staircases.

How the Double Helix Works

The clever part is the double helix. Instead of one spiral, there are two, wound around each other but never touching. One ramp is for going up and the other for coming down, so the crowds flow smoothly without people meeting head-on.

The ramps are shallow and wide, which is why the staircase feels so graceful rather than steep. Looking straight down the middle, the two spirals line up into a single continuous curl, which is the view that makes the famous photograph.

The Original Bramante Staircase

The staircase that inspired Momo is the original Bramante Staircase, designed by Donato Bramante around 1505 for Pope Julius II. It is a single spiral ramp, built so that mules and servants could carry goods up to the papal apartments without using steps.

This original is in a different part of the Vatican and is not on the normal visitor route. You can only see it on certain special or early-access tours.

So when people talk about the famous spiral staircase they have photographed at the exit, they almost always mean Momo's 1932 version, not Bramante's original.

Why It Is So Famous

The staircase is famous for its beauty and its symmetry. The double spiral is both a piece of clever engineering and a genuinely lovely thing to look at, and it photographs better than almost anything else in the museums. It regularly appears on lists of the most beautiful staircases in the world.

It also helps that it comes at the end of the visit, a calm, striking final image after the crowds and the grandeur of the galleries. For many visitors it is the photo they remember the trip by.

How to See and Photograph It

You will see the staircase automatically as you leave, so the only real question is how to get a clean photo:

  • Shoot straight down - Stand at the top and point your camera down the centre of the spiral for the full double-helix effect.
  • Go early - Like the rest of the museums, the exit is quietest at the start of the day, as covered in our best time to visit guide. Later on, a steady stream of people fills the ramps.
  • No flash - You can take photos here without flash, just as in the galleries. Our Vatican Museums photos guide covers the best spots and the rules.
  • Have your camera ready - It is the last thing you see, so do not pack your phone away too soon.

To reach the staircase, you simply visit the museums. Book a timed skip-the-line entry ticket for an early slot to walk the galleries, the Sistine Chapel, and reach the staircase before the crowds build.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where is the spiral staircase in the Vatican Museums?

The spiral staircase is at the exit, at the very end of the visit. After the Sistine Chapel, your route brings you back toward the entrance hall, and the staircase is the last thing you descend on the way out.

Who designed the Vatican Museums spiral staircase?

The Italian architect Giuseppe Momo, in 1932. He was inspired by the older Bramante Staircase of 1505, which is why the modern one is sometimes called the Bramante Staircase as well.

Is the spiral staircase the Bramante Staircase?

Not exactly. The famous one at the exit is Momo's 1932 staircase, inspired by Bramante's original from 1505. The original Bramante Staircase is in another part of the Vatican and is only seen on special tours.

Why is the Vatican spiral staircase famous?

For its double-helix design and its beauty. Two spirals wind around each other so people going up and down never meet, and the view looking down the centre is one of the most photographed images in the Vatican.

Can you take photos of the spiral staircase?

Yes, without flash. It is one of the best photo spots in the museums, and the classic shot is taken looking straight down from the top.

Do I need a separate ticket to see the spiral staircase?

No. It is part of the standard museum route at the exit, so every Vatican Museums ticket includes it.
