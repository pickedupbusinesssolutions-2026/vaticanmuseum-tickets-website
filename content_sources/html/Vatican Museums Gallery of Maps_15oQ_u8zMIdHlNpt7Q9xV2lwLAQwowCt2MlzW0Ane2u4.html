The Vatican Museums Gallery of Maps is the "golden tunnel" you have seen all over social media, a long corridor that glows with gold and colour and stops almost everyone in their tracks.

What is the Gallery of Maps?

The Gallery of Maps, or Galleria delle Carte Geografiche in Italian, is a long corridor in the Vatican Museums, about 120 metres from end to end and 6 metres wide. Its walls carry 40 large frescoed maps showing the regions of Italy and the territories the Church held at the time.

It was built as a grand connecting gallery and as a statement of knowledge and power, laying out the whole of Italy for the pope to walk through. Today it is one of the most striking rooms on the visitor route, and for many the highlight after the Sistine Chapel.

Where is it in the Vatican Museums?

The gallery comes near the end of the one-way route, after the long galleries of tapestries and candelabra and shortly before the Raphael Rooms and the Sistine Chapel. By this point you are close to the climax of the visit, which is why the corridor is usually busy.

It is included in every Vatican Museums ticket, as part of the route, so there is nothing separate to book. Our Raphael Rooms guide covers what comes next, just before the chapel.

The Maps on the Walls

The 40 maps run the length of both walls, and they are arranged with a clever logic. The spine of Italy is the Apennine mountains, so the maps on one side show the regions that drain toward the western, Tyrrhenian coast, and those on the other show the regions facing the eastern, Adriatic side.

Each map is a detailed, bird's-eye view of a region or city, complete with towns, rivers, and coastlines, and many include small scenes of famous events. For their day they were astonishingly accurate, and they still read clearly as maps of places you can recognise.

The Ceiling

If the maps draw you in, the ceiling is what makes people stop and look up. The vault of the Gallery of Maps is covered in gilded stucco and bright frescoes, divided into dozens of panels showing religious scenes linked to the region mapped on the wall below.

The effect is a long tunnel of gold that seems to glow, and it is the real reason the room photographs so well.

It is worth walking slowly and looking up as you go, since the ceiling changes along the length of the corridor rather than repeating.

Who Made it and When

The gallery was commissioned by Pope Gregory XIII and painted in a remarkably short time, between 1580 and 1583. The maps were based on the careful drawings of Ignazio Danti, a Dominican friar who was also a mathematician and one of the leading geographers of the age.

The painting itself was carried out by a large team of artists working under the direction of Girolamo Muziano and Cesare Nebbia. That so much was completed in about three years is part of what makes the gallery so impressive.

Why it is the Most Photographed Room

The Gallery of Maps competes with the spiral staircase as the single most photographed spot in the Vatican Museums.

The combination of the long straight corridor, the glowing gold ceiling, and the symmetry makes for a dramatic shot, especially looking straight down the length of the hall.

The catch is that everyone else wants the same photo. By mid-morning the corridor is shoulder to shoulder, and a clear shot is almost impossible. Our Vatican Museums photos guide shows the best spots and when to catch them empty.

Visiting the Gallery of Maps

There is nothing to book separately. Any Vatican Museums ticket includes the Gallery of Maps, and you walk through it on the way to the Sistine Chapel. A few tips:

  • Go early - The first entry of the day is the only realistic time to see the corridor without a crowd. Our best time to visit guide has the quiet windows.
  • Look up - The ceiling is the best part, and it is easy to miss while watching the maps or the crowd.
  • Photos are fine - You can take photos without flash in the gallery, unlike in the Sistine Chapel.

To walk it at a steady pace, book a timed skip-the-line entry ticket for an early slot, or take a guided tour that explains the maps and the ceiling as you go.
