What is Pinacoteca?
The Pinacoteca, which simply means picture gallery in Italian, is the Vatican's collection of paintings. It lives in its own purpose-built hall that opened in 1932, with 18 rooms holding around 460 works.
The rooms run in order of time, starting with gold-ground panels from the Middle Ages and moving through the Renaissance and Baroque to the 19th century. That makes it easy to follow the story of painting as you walk, from stiff medieval altarpieces to the drama of Caravaggio.
Where is Pinacoteca in the Vatican Museums?
The Pinacoteca is set slightly apart from the main one-way route, in its own building near the entrance. Because it is off to the side, rather than on the direct path to the Sistine Chapel, most visitors miss it, which is exactly why it stays so peaceful.
You can visit it at the start of your trip, before the galleries, or as a detour on the way round. Either way it is included in your ticket, so there is nothing extra to book. Our museum map shows where it sits.
The Must-See Paintings
A few works make the detour worthwhile on their own:
* Raphael's Transfiguration - Raphael's last painting, and the star of the gallery, in the room devoted to him.
* Leonardo da Vinci's St. Jerome in the Wilderness - An unfinished work, rare and haunting, showing the saint in the desert.
* Caravaggio's Entombment - A powerful, dramatic scene of Christ being laid in the tomb, lit in Caravaggio's unmistakable style.
* Giotto's Stefaneschi Triptych - A beautiful early-14th-century altarpiece, painted on both sides.
* Melozzo da Forlì's musician angels - Gentle fresco fragments of angels playing instruments, among the most charming things in the museums.
The gallery also has works by Fra Angelico, Bellini, Titian, Perugino, and Guido Reni, so there is far more than can be listed here.
Raphael's Transfiguration
The Transfiguration is the painting most people come to the Pinacoteca to see. It was Raphael's final work, left not quite finished when he died in 1520, and completed by his workshop. It shows two scenes at once: Christ rising in light above, and the disciples struggling with a possessed boy below. 
It hangs in the room devoted to Raphael, alongside his Madonna of Foligno and the large tapestry designs he made for the Sistine Chapel. For anyone who has walked the Raphael Rooms earlier in the museums, it is a fitting final word from the same artist.
Why it is Worth the Detour
The Pinacoteca offers two things the rest of the museums struggle to: masterpieces you can actually stand in front of, and quiet. While the Gallery of Maps and the Sistine Chapel are shoulder to shoulder, you can often have a Caravaggio or a Leonardo almost to yourself here.
It suits anyone who loves paintings, wants a calm break from the crowds, or simply has the time to see more than the headline sights. If you are rushing through in under two hours, you may choose to skip it, but if you can spare 30 to 45 minutes, it rewards them.
Visiting the Pinacoteca
There is nothing separate to book, as the Pinacoteca is part of the standard Vatican Museums ticket. A few tips:
* Do it first or last - Visit at the start before the crowds build, or at the end as a calm finish, rather than fighting the flow toward the Sistine Chapel.
* Allow 30 to 45 minutes - That is enough for the highlights without rushing.
* Enjoy the quiet - It is one of the few places in the museums where you can slow down, so make the most of it.
To see the Pinacoteca along with the rest, book a timed skip-the-line entry ticket for an early slot, or take a guided tour if you want the paintings explained.
